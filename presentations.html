Presentation Topics Questions without answer keys

Fourteen topics on The French Lieutenant’s Woman (FLW) and The Ebony Tower, each with guiding questions.

Fowles hands his readers a choice: which ending, which reading, which kind of freedom. The guiding questions do the same. You will not find their answers in a textbook. They ask you to take a position, to justify it from the text, and to notice what it says about your own expectations.

How to use the questionsTreat them as a starting point, not a checklist. Your presentation should still answer the topic itself with close readings and secondary literature. But choose at least one question to which you give your own, argued answer, and bring one of them into the discussion with the group.
The Role of Science (and Voyeurism) in FLWFLW
  1. Charles studies fossils, the narrator studies Charles, and both study Sarah. When does looking at someone in order to understand them turn into a way of possessing them? Where would you draw that line in the way you observe other people?
  2. Is classifying a person, as a type, a case or a diagnosis, a way of coming closer to them or of keeping them at a distance? Think of a moment when you were classified, or classified someone yourself.
  3. Reading a novel is a kind of watching too. What do you, as a reader, want to see of Sarah, and what does that wish say about you?

Starting point: Subject and anti-humanism

Foucault’s Panopticon and FLWFLW
  1. Who watches whom in Lyme Regis, and how far do the watched begin to watch themselves? Which of your own habits exist mainly because someone might be looking?
  2. Is a narrator who sees into every character a guard in the tower? Would you rather read a novel whose narrator sees everything, or one who admits blind spots, and why?
  3. Sarah is watched all the time, yet nobody seems to see her. Can being visible and being unknown go together? Where have you experienced this, for instance online?

Starting point: Foucault and Bentham’s Panopticon in the further links

Stereotypes of Femininity in FLW: Feminist / Witch / ExistentialistFLW
  1. Which of the three labels did you instinctively reach for when you first met Sarah, and what in your own expectations made it the obvious one?
  2. Can a novel describe a woman without drawing on any stereotype at all? What would a description look like that you would accept for yourself?
  3. A man wrote this novel in 1969 about a woman in 1867. Whose idea of a free woman is Sarah: her own, the narrator’s, Fowles’s or yours?
Sarah Woodruff: Protagonist and Heroine or Means to an End?FLW
  1. Whose story is this in the end, Sarah’s or Charles’s? At which point in the text did your answer change, if it did?
  2. If Sarah exists above all to set Charles free, does that diminish her, or can serving another person’s development be a form of freedom too? Think of someone who was a “means to an end” in your own life, or of a time when you were one.
  3. Imagine one key scene told from Sarah’s point of view. What would be lost, and what would be gained?

Starting point: Character (Sinfield on Desdemona)

Chapter 13 in FLW: Postmodern Writing and MetafictionFLW
  1. How did you react when the narrator stepped out of the story and declared it imagined: irritated, relieved, curious? What does your reaction reveal about what you expect from a novel?
  2. The narrator claims his characters disobey him. In what sense, if any, can a character be free? And in what sense are you yourself “written” by others?
  3. Does knowing that something is constructed spoil it for you? Think of other things you know to be constructed and still believe in.
The Meaning of the Three Endings in FLWFLW
  1. Which ending did you want, which one do you believe, and why might those two be different?
  2. If an author offers several endings, who is responsible for the one that counts? How do you deal with decisions in your own life where you would like to keep several endings open?
  3. The last ending is reached by setting a watch back a quarter of an hour. If you could replay fifteen minutes of a decision of your own, would a different ending be truer, or only different?
The Philosophical Dimension of FLW (Existentialism / Humanism)FLW
  1. Is Charles free at the end, or merely abandoned? Where do you draw the line between freedom and loss?
  2. Sartre holds us responsible even for what we do out of habit or duty. Name one thing you do “because you have to”. Would Sartre accept your reason?
  3. Does the novel treat its characters as players or as pawns? And does reading it make you feel more like the one or the other?

Starting point: Existentialism and player or pawn

Conceptions of Femininity: The Mouse, the Freak, the HousewifeEbony Tower
  1. A nickname reduces a person to a single trait. Who gives these names, and what do the names spare the person using them from having to see?
  2. Of the three roles, “the housewife” sounds the most conventional. Is she the least free of the three women, or is there a kind of freedom in that role which the text, or you, overlook?
  3. Which of the three roles would you least like to be cast in, and why?
Conceptions of Masculinity: David Williams and Henry BreasleyEbony Tower
  1. One man is old, provocative and unrestrained, the other young, polite and careful. Which kind of man does the text seem to admire, and do you share that admiration?
  2. Is holding back a sign of moral strength or a failure of nerve? Think of a moment when you held back yourself. How do you judge it today?
  3. Which expectations of masculinity, or of being a “proper” adult, do you recognize in yourself as limiting?
There’s Always a Price to Pay: Art, Sexuality and Morality in “The Ebony Tower”Ebony Tower
  1. Does great art require breaking moral rules? Name an artist whose work you admire despite, or because of, the way he or she lived. How do you deal with that tension?
  2. What price does David pay for the choice he makes, and what price would he have paid for the other one? Which price would you rather pay?
  3. Is a safe, decent life compatible with real creativity? What does your answer reveal about how you want to live?
Nature vs Culture, or: How Natural is Breasley’s Lost Domain?Ebony Tower
  1. Coëtminais looks like a place outside society, yet it is owned, run and in a sense staged. Where do you go to feel “natural”, and how much culture does that place actually contain?
  2. Is Breasley’s naturalness authentic or a pose? How would you tell the difference in another person, or in yourself?
  3. Why might a reader today long for such a lost domain? What would you have to give up to live there?
The Body as a Last Stronghold of Authenticity in “The Ebony Tower”?Ebony Tower
  1. When everything else has become theory, technique and talk, is the body the one place where we cannot lie? Or does the body lie as well?
  2. In which situations do you trust your body more than your thoughts, and has that trust ever misled you?
  3. If the subject is constructed, as anti-humanist theory says, is the body constructed too? What would follow for the idea of authenticity?

Starting point: Being-in-the-world (“I am my body”)

Intertextuality: The Meaning of the Medieval Courtly Romance for “The Ebony Tower”Ebony Tower
  1. Courtly romance sends a knight into an enchanted forest to be tested by love and adventure. Which elements of that pattern do you recognize in David’s visit, and does the old pattern make his story larger or smaller?
  2. In the collection, Fowles’s translation of Marie de France’s “Eliduc” follows the novella. Why might he have wanted the two to be read together? Does reading old stories change the way you see your own relationships?
  3. Does knowing the old script behind a story make a modern experience less personal, or easier to understand? Think of a moment when your own life seemed to follow a script.
Intermediality: The Importance of the International Gothic (Uccello / Pisanello) for “The Ebony Tower”Ebony Tower
  1. Look at Uccello’s The Hunt in the Forest before you read David’s critique of Breasley’s painting. What do you notice first, and how does your way of seeing differ from his?
  2. Why might a modern painter turn back to the art of the fifteenth century? Which older art, music or writing speaks to you more than contemporary work, and why?
  3. Can a painting do something a text cannot? Which medium do you trust more to tell the truth about a feeling?
