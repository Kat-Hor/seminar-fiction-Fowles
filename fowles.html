John Fowles 1926–2005

An English novelist, critically positioned between modernism and postmodernism, whose work was influenced by Jean-Paul Sartre and Albert Camus, among others.

At a glance

The end Fowles’s fiction keeps asking who decides how a story ends: the author, the characters or the reader.

After leaving Oxford, Fowles taught English on the Greek island of Spetses, a sojourn that inspired The Magus (1965), an instant best-seller in tune with 1960s “hippy” anarchism and experimental philosophy. It was followed by The French Lieutenant’s Woman (1969), a Victorian-era romance with a postmodern twist, set in Lyme Regis, Dorset, where Fowles lived for much of his life. His books have been translated into many languages, and several have been adapted as films.

Early life

Anarchy Leaving the Marines, Fowles decided to become “a sort of anarchist”.

Birth and family

Fowles was born in Leigh-on-Sea in Essex, the only son and elder child of Robert John Fowles and Gladys May, née Richards; his sister Hazel was born fifteen years later. His father had trained as a lawyer but worked for the family business, the tobacco importer Allen & Wright.

More on the familyThe tobacco firm, the First World War and Westcliff-on-Sea

Robert Fowles’s father Reginald had been a partner in Allen & Wright. At Reginald’s death Robert was obliged to run the firm: his brother had died in the Battle of Ypres, and there were young half-siblings from his father’s second marriage to provide for.

Gladys was the daughter of John Richards, a draper, and his wife Elizabeth, who was in service. The family came from Cornwall to London, where John became chief buyer for a department store, and gave their daughter a “comfortable upbringing in Chelsea”. After the Spanish flu pandemic of 1918 they moved to Westcliff-on-Sea in Essex for the healthier climate.

Robert Fowles returned from the First World War in bad health after three years as an officer in the Honourable Artillery Company, and met his future wife at a tennis club in Westcliff-on-Sea.

School

As a child Fowles was a close companion of his mother, and of his cousin Peggy, eighteen years his senior, until he was ten. He attended Alleyn Court Preparatory School, where a maternal uncle and aunt taught. In 1939 he won a place at Bedford School, where he stayed until 1944, became head boy and stood out as an athlete, captaining the cricket team.

The Marines and Oxford

After Bedford he took a Naval Short Course at the University of Edinburgh and trained for a commission in the Royal Marines. He completed his training on 8 May 1945 and spent the next two years at Okehampton Camp in Devon.

In 1947 he entered New College, Oxford, to study French and German; he later concentrated on French and graduated in 1950 with second-class honours. He was going through a political transformation at the time:

“I … began to hate what I was becoming in life—a British Establishment young hopeful. I decided instead to become a sort of anarchist.”John Fowles on leaving the Marines

At Oxford he first considered life as a writer, above all after reading existentialists such as Jean-Paul Sartre and Albert Camus. The existentialist notions of “authenticity” and “freedom” that pervaded Oxford influenced him. Fowles did not identify as an existentialist, but their writing grew out of a feeling that the world was absurd, and he shared that feeling.

For the seminarAuthenticity and freedom are the two words to keep in mind for this whole course. The page on existentialism explains where they come from.

Teaching and Greece

The magus A magician or sage: the title of the novel that grew out of Fowles’s years on Spetses.

Fowles spent his early adult life as a teacher. His first year after Oxford was spent at the University of Poitiers. He then had two offers, one from the French department at Winchester College, the other “from a ratty school in Greece”:

“Of course, I went against all the dictates of common sense and took the Greek job.”

In 1951 he became English master at the Anargyrios and Korgialenios School on Spetses, an island off the Peloponnese. It opened a critical period in his life: here he met his future wife, and his experiences and feelings on the island became the setting of The Magus. Fowles was happy in Greece, especially outside the school. He wrote poems, which he later published, and became close to his fellow expatriates. In 1953 he and the other masters were dismissed for trying to introduce reforms, and he returned to England.

ElizabethSpetses, separation and marriage in 1957

On Spetses Fowles began a relationship with Elizabeth Christy, née Whitton, who was then married to another teacher, Roy Christy; that marriage was ending because of Fowles. Although they returned to England at the same time, they were at first no longer in each other’s company. It was in this period that Fowles began drafting The Magus.

The separation did not last long. They married on 2 April 1957, and Fowles became stepfather to Elizabeth’s daughter Anna.

London

For nearly ten years Fowles then taught English as a foreign language at St Godric’s College, an all-girls establishment in Hampstead, London.

Literary career

The best The meaning of “aristos”, which Fowles chose as the title of his book of philosophical essays.

The Collector (1963)

In late 1960, though he had already drafted The Magus, Fowles began work on The Collector. He finished the first draft in a month but spent more than a year revising it. Michael S. Howard of Jonathan Cape was enthusiastic, and when the paperback rights were sold in 1963 it was, in Howard’s words, “probably the highest price that had hitherto been paid for a first novel”. British reviewers read it as an innovative thriller; several American critics detected a serious promotion of existentialist thought. The success meant Fowles could give up teaching. The film adaptation followed in 1965.

The Aristos (1964) and The Magus (1965)

Against his publisher’s advice, Fowles insisted that his second book be The Aristos, a non-fiction collection of philosophical essays. He then collated the many drafts of what would become his most studied work, The Magus. In 1968 he adapted it for the cinema. The film was generally considered awful; asked whether he would change anything if he could live his life again, Peter Sellers joked that he would do everything exactly the same “except I wouldn’t see The Magus”.

The French Lieutenant’s Woman (1969)

In 1965 Fowles left London for Underhill, a farm on the edge of Lyme Regis. The isolated farmhouse became the model for the Dairy in the novel he was writing. Finding the farm too remote (“total solitude gets a bit monotonous”), he and his wife moved in 1968 to Belmont in Lyme Regis, a house once owned by Eleanor Coade, which he also used as a setting.

In this novel Fowles created one of the most enigmatic female characters in literary history. His conception of femininity and his myth of masculinity, as developed in this text, are psychoanalytically informed. The novel was a critical and popular success, was translated into more than ten languages and established his international reputation. In 1981 it was filmed with a screenplay by Harold Pinter, starring Meryl Streep and Jeremy Irons.

The later works

The Ebony Tower (1974), Daniel Martin (1977), Mantissa (1982) and A Maggot (1985) were all written at Belmont House. In 1980 he wrote an appreciative introduction to G. B. Edwards’s The Book of Ebenezer Le Page (1981), a fictional autobiography set in Guernsey. Fowles also wrote poems and short stories throughout his life, most of which were lost or destroyed. In 2008 The Times named him one of the fifty greatest British writers since 1945.

Lyme Regis and later life

Fossils Lyme Regis is famous for its ammonites, and collecting fossils is Charles Smithson’s hobby.

Fowles lived in Lyme Regis for the rest of his life. From 1979 to 1988 he was curator of the Lyme Regis Museum, retiring after a mild stroke. He was occasionally involved in local politics and wrote letters to The Times in favour of preservation, but he was generally considered reclusive.

In 1990 his wife Elizabeth died of cancer, only a week after the diagnosis. Her death affected him severely, and he did not write for a year. In 1998 the New York Times Book Review quoted him:

“Being an atheist is a matter not of moral choice, but of human obligation.”

In 1998 he married his second wife, Sarah Smith. With her by his side he died of heart failure on 5 November 2005, aged 79, in Axminster Hospital.

The letters to Elena van LieshoutSold at Sotheby’s in 2008

In 2008 Elena van Lieshout offered 120 love letters and postcards for auction at Sotheby’s. The correspondence began in 1990, when Fowles was 65. She was a young Welsh admirer and a student at St Hilda’s College, Oxford, who had contacted the reclusive author, and the two developed a sensitive, though unconsummated, relationship.
