Key Concepts Freedom, the human, the self

Existentialism, humanism and the theoretical debate about the subject and literary character: the philosophical horizon of Fowles’s fiction.

Overview

Three debates meet in Fowles’s fiction. Existentialism asks how a human being can live freely and authentically in a world without given meaning. Humanism places human beings, their capacities and their self-determination at the centre. And much literary theory since the 1960s has attacked both: for anti-humanism the subject is not a sovereign source of meaning but a site shaped by language, ideology and the unconscious, and literary character looks correspondingly less solid.

The humanist view

“Man can be a player.” The individual is autonomous, capable of self-determination, and his or her choices can make a real difference to society and history.

The anti-humanist view

“Man can only be a pawn in the game of life.” Social, economic and psychological structures determine how individuals act; self-determination is an illusion.

Both formulations: Thomas Mautner, “Humanism”, A Dictionary of Philosophy (Oxford: Blackwell, 1996).

For the seminarA novel that offers its readers several endings, and whose narrator claims he cannot control his characters, stages exactly this question: player or pawn? And who plays: the characters, the author or the reader?

Existentialism

Anxiety For Heidegger and Sartre not despair, but a symptom of freedom and self-awareness.

Existentialism is a philosophical movement usually traced back to the Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard. The term itself was first introduced by Gabriel Marcel in 1943 and then taken up by Jean-Paul Sartre; Karl Jaspers, from the same tradition, had earlier spoken of “existence philosophy”. The existentialists differ widely, and given their individualism many of them denied belonging to any “movement” at all.

The human conditionAfter Charles B. Guignon

Existentialists start from the assumption that it is no longer possible to believe in a transcendent justification for our existence. We seek an overarching meaning for our lives, but there is no “proper function of humans” and no “plan in God’s mind” that tells us the right way to be human.

Instead of thinking of a human being as a thing (a mind, a body or both), existentialists describe a deep tension between two sides of our being. We are organisms with needs and drives, living at the level of sensation and desire. But because we are capable of self-awareness, we can reflect on our desires and evaluate ourselves in terms of a larger vision of what our lives are adding up to. The self is therefore not an object but an unfolding event: “I am what I make of myself throughout the course of my life as a whole.”

Being-in-the-world and facticityWhy there is no “view from nowhere”

Existentialists distrust abstract theorizing about humans. They begin with our everyday situation as agents, before reflection. It is part of our facticity that we are caught up in the midst of things, involved with others, affected by moods and commitments, embodied (“I am my body”, as Gabriel Marcel wrote, a theme central to Simone de Beauvoir and Maurice Merleau-Ponty) and embedded in a particular cultural and historical milieu.

So there is no way to achieve a “God’s-eye view” of ourselves. A totally detached standpoint, which Marcel calls “desertion” and Merleau-Ponty “high-altitude thinking”, distorts the world because it bleaches out our sense of what matters.

Freedom and responsibility“Condemned to be free”

Our choices are free in three senses: no outside factors determine our will; in any particular case we could have acted otherwise; and we are therefore responsible for our choices in a way that justifies praise and blame.

Even habitual actions are choices. Someone who goes to work every day because he must support his family is, for Sartre, deceiving himself if he thinks he is compelled: he could walk away and join a monastery. Letting duty or money decide is itself a choice, through which he takes on an identity and for which he is responsible. Sartre captures this in the formula that humans are “condemned to be free”.

Nothingness, the Absurd and its transcendenceAfter Richard Sheppard

Existentialist writing begins from the experience of nothingness, anomie and absurdity. A dimension of Being has been forced out of consciousness by societies that overvalue rationality, will-power, acquisitiveness, productivity and technical skill. Individuals feel thrown into a world of fragments that say nothing, among people who talk past each other, in a stream of disconnected moments. Nietzsche called this experience “the death of God”; Kafka’s The Castle (1926) is perhaps its fullest literary expression.

Yet the paradox at the heart of existentialist psychology is that this Angst points to its own transcendence: precisely because we experience absurdity so intensely, there must be a human propensity towards order and meaning. Sartre equates it with the will, Camus leaves it unnamed, Heidegger calls it Sorge (care), Kierkegaard the soul.

Sartre’s dictum “existence precedes essence” can therefore mean two things: either that existence is meaningless and we must create our own values, or that a hidden meaning is embedded in each existence and must be discovered and lived.

Sources: Robert Solomon, “Existentialism”, in Mautner, A Dictionary of Philosophy (1996); Richard Sheppard, “Existentialism”, in The Routledge Dictionary of Literary Terms (2006); Charles B. Guignon, “Existentialism”, Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy, vol. 3 (1998).

The thinkers

The absurd Camus’s term, which has (wrongly) come to stand for existentialism as a whole.

Kierkegaard was a devout Christian, Nietzsche an atheist, Sartre a Marxist, and Heidegger joined the Nazi party in 1933. Kierkegaard and Sartre insisted on the freedom of the will, Nietzsche denied it, Heidegger hardly discussed it. What they share is an attitude particularly relevant to modern mass society. Turn the cards.

Religious existentialists inspired by Kierkegaard include Paul Tillich, Martin Buber, Karl Barth and Gabriel Marcel. For Buber, the “eternal Thou” breaks into time through the “human Thou” (I and Thou, 1923); for Jaspers, people can listen in “philosophical faith” for a hidden Transcendence.

For the seminarFowles read Sartre and Camus at Oxford and shared their sense of the absurd, but never called himself an existentialist. Look out for which of these positions his narrators and characters actually take.

Humanism

The humanities Grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy: the Renaissance curriculum at the root of humanism.

Humanism is less a school of thought than a general perspective that assigns human beings a special place in the scheme of things. It occupies a middle position between two competitors: an emphasis on the supernatural, which makes humanity radically dependent on a divine order, and a scientific view that treats humans as one organism among others. Humanism sees in human beings unique capacities to be cultivated and celebrated for their own sake.

Three meanings

From the studia humanitatis to the word “humanism”Renaissance Italy and a 19th-century coinage

The Renaissance (roughly 1350 to 1650) had no word “humanism”. It had the Latin phrase studia humanitatis, borrowed from classical antiquity: Cicero used it a few times, and the grammarian Aulus Gellius equated humanitas with the Greek παιδεία (paideia), the liberal education meant to develop a child’s intellectual, moral and aesthetic capacities. A teacher of these subjects was called umanista, apparently first in Italian student slang. The dominant discipline was rhetoric, and eloquence was the humanists’ highest accomplishment.

The word Humanismus was coined in the early nineteenth century by the German educator F. J. Niethammer, for an education based on the Greek and Latin classics, and then borrowed by historians such as Georg Voigt (1859) to describe the Renaissance movement.

Renaissance humanism was neither a philosophy nor an ideology: some humanists were pious and some not, some became Protestants and others remained Catholic. Its lasting effect was the recovery and translation of a great reservoir of Greek texts. As long as we value literature and history, every educated person is, in this sense, a humanist.

Enlightenment and afterPerfectibility, and its doubters

Eighteenth-century thinkers such as Voltaire, Diderot, Rousseau, Bentham, Hume, Lessing, Kant, Franklin and Jefferson, though not always agreeing, formed a family united in support of freedom, equality, tolerance, secularism and cosmopolitanism. They believed in the perfectibility of human nature, in moral sense and responsibility, and in progress.

In the nineteenth century Marx, Nietzsche and Freud began to question human autonomy: Marx showed that humans are conditioned by class, Nietzsche that they are still caught in Christian morality, Freud that they are torn between instinct and ideal. Saussure’s insight, developed by Foucault, Lacan and Derrida, that language shapes us more than we shape language, undermined a further humanist assumption.

The image of the human in Renaissance artDonatello, Leonardo, Michelangelo

Even when painting religious scenes, Renaissance artists celebrated the human body and gave it intrinsic value and dignity. Examples: Donatello’s bronze David (probably 1440s, Bargello, Florence), Leonardo’s Mona Lisa (begun c. 1503, Louvre) and Michelangelo’s Creation of Adam (c. 1511) on the Sistine Chapel ceiling.

Medieval thinkers, it has been said, philosophized on their knees; the humanists dared to stand up. Instead of the passive “Everyman” of medieval drama, fought over by a good and a bad angel, the Renaissance placed the distinctive, self-determining individual.

Sources: Konstantin Kolenda, “Humanism”, The Cambridge Dictionary of Philosophy; Thomas Mautner, “Humanism” (1996); John C. Luik, “Humanism”, and John Monfasani, “Humanism, Renaissance”, Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy, vol. 4 (1998); Gary Day, “Humanism”, The Routledge Dictionary of Literary Terms (2006).

Subject and anti-humanism

The thinking self The subject as self or ego, the main target of recent theory.

In recent theory the traditional “subject”, the self or ego, has been used in a largely pejorative sense. Two beliefs came under attack: that the human subject is a point of origin for historical, social or even personal events, and that the individual has valid self-knowledge and is in charge of him- or herself.

The traditional subject

Primary, unified, self-present, self-determining, autonomous, homogeneous: the source and master of meaning.

The post-structuralist subject

Secondary, constructed (by language or ideology), volatile, “standing in its own shadow”, self-divided: a site where things happen rather than that which makes them happen.

Barthes, Foucault, Culler, LacanThe dissolution of the subject

In Roland Barthes’s “The Death of the Author” it is language that displaces the individual, who reappears, however, as the reader. Michel Foucault, in “What is an Author?”, goes further: the subject is to be deprived of its role as originator and analysed “as a variable and complex function of discourse”.

Jonathan Culler: as the self is broken down into component systems, it comes to seem more and more like a construct, “a result of systems of convention”; the “I” is not given but comes to exist as that which is addressed by others. An influential figure here is the psychoanalyst Jacques Lacan, with his writing on the mirror stage.

Althusser and the turn against humanismAnd why some critics want humanism back

The pejorative use of “humanism” in Anglo-American theory can probably be dated from Louis Althusser’s For Marx (French 1965, English 1969). For Althusser, positing a human essence is the heart of humanism. His followers call humanism idealist, ahistorical and individualistic: it locates a timeless human essence in individuals rather than in social structures, and often projects the features of one society (hence “bourgeois” or “liberal” humanism) onto humanity at large.

Critics of this critique answer that anti-humanism throws the baby out with the bathwater. Francis Mulhern points out that anti-humanism, too, belongs to a tradition that affirms human self-development, and that “too euphoric a dissolution of the humanist subject” may reduce all emancipation to “a Babel” of positional gambits. Michèle Barrett recalls the progressive role of humanism as a secularizing force. A non-essentialist humanism could offer relatively stable, if contested, reference points for “the human”.

Source: Jeremy Hawthorn, A Glossary of Contemporary Literary Theory (London: Arnold), entries “Subject and subjectivity”, “Character” and “Humanism”.

Character

Character Originally a stamp or imprint, the mark pressed into a coin.

The concept of literary character has had a hard time in twentieth-century theory. If the human being is more site than source, “more puppet or response than puppet-master or cause”, then literary character has less claim to serious attention. Theorists here follow modernist and postmodernist writers who had abandoned traditional characterization.

“You mustn’t look in my novel for the old stable ego of the character.”D. H. Lawrence, letter to Edward Garnett, 1914

Lawrence still believed in a single human element underneath, which passes through “allotropic states” (allotropy: a change of physical properties without a change of substance). Later novelists abandoned even this belief: the unity of the individual, real or literary, is an illusion; human beings are sites of struggle and division.

Lyotard, Prince, SinfieldNodal points, anthropomorphic existents and Desdemona

Jean-François Lyotard (The Postmodern Condition): a self “does not amount to much, but no self is an island”; each person is located at “nodal points” of communication circuits, a post through which messages pass.

Gerald Prince defines character as “an existent endowed with anthropomorphic traits and engaged in anthropomorphic actions”. Alan Sinfield argues that Desdemona “is a disjointed sequence of positions that women are conventionally supposed to occupy” and has no character of her own, being a convenience in the story of Othello, Iago and Venice; by implication, others in the play do possess one.

For the seminarSinfield’s question can be asked of Fowles’s women: is Sarah Woodruff a character in her own right, or a sequence of positions that serve Charles’s story? This is one of the presentation topics.
